# Visual Direction

## Start From the Product

Before choosing a look, write a one-page inventory:

- what the page is for and what the person does next;
- the objects and data the page shows, with real units and magnitudes;
- the product's own vocabulary, symbols, materials or workflow;
- what the person already knows from the previous screen;
- what the target profile can afford.

A direction that could belong to any settings page is not a direction. Pick one
thing from the inventory that only this product has, and make it the page's
organising idea. It can be a data relationship, a physical object, a workflow,
a place, a sound, or a way of showing progress.

## Direction Statement

Write exactly one sentence before choosing tokens:

> On this page, **[something from the product]** becomes **[the composition or
> interaction device]**.

If the second blank is “a card grid”, “a hero section” or “a gradient”, the idea
is a template. Try again.

## Choose a Direction, Not a Trend

Use the selected profile's style axes as constraints, not as a menu of trends:

- `surface_model` decides whether hierarchy comes from flat spacing, tonal
  steps, elevation or outlines.
- `depth_model` decides whether depth is border, lightness, shadow or absent.
- `density` decides how much information belongs above the fold.
- `corner_scale`, `type_scale`, `icon_style` and `color_temperature` carry the
  personality.
- `decoration` is a budget, not an instruction to decorate.

Do not blend two profiles. If the brief asks for “like X but with Y”, create a
new profile through `design-contract`, then use it consistently.

## Two-Pass Review

Before implementation:

1. Write the direction statement and a compact plan.
2. Compare it with a generic version you would produce for any similar product.
3. Remove the generic device. If the plan still reads as a template, change the
   composition, not the palette.

## Anti-Template Checklist

Flag these unless the product genuinely requires them:

- identical cards repeated for unequal content;
- a hero-metric block with a big number, small label and accent;
- an eyebrow label above every heading;
- numbered sections that are not a sequence;
- a gradient, glow or blur used as the only sign of quality;
- the same radius on every surface regardless of hierarchy;
- three feature cards with icon, title and two lines of filler;
- invented statistics or fake data;
- emoji as structural icons.

## Operate Surfaces

For settings, tools, lists and status pages, familiarity beats novelty. The
page should disappear into the task. Spend expression on one precise detail:
a strong title, a meaningful progress form, a well-made empty state, or a
single accent that marks the current action. Everything else stays quiet.
